Research

My work concerns performance at the boundary between software and hardware — how a compiler maps a program onto the machine that runs it, and what is lost when that mapping ignores the machine's structure. Three undergraduate projects, in compilers and in machine learning, are described below, followed by the work I am pursuing now.

Research Interests

I am interested in compilers that are aware of the hardware they target: how a compiler should reason about memory topology, dataflow and scheduling rather than treating the machine as a flat, uniform device. Three threads run through my work:

  • Hardware-aware compilation. Memory placement, locality and thread affinity as first-class compiler concerns, rather than runtime afterthoughts.
  • Dataflow and reconfigurable architectures. Compiling high-level programs into dynamically scheduled circuits, where the schedule is decided by data availability instead of a fixed state machine.
  • AI inference optimization. Making large-model inference efficient on commodity and specialized hardware, especially where memory bandwidth, not arithmetic, is the limit.

NUMA-Torch

Compiler-Driven NUMA-Aware LLM Prefill on Multi-Socket CPUs

Jun 2025 – Jun 2026

B.Sc. thesis · advised by Prof. Sanath Jayasena (University of Moratuwa) and Dr. Tharindu R. Patabandi (Qualcomm Technologies) · team lead

Problem
Large language model prefill on multi-socket CPUs is DRAM-bound. TorchInductor places weights without any awareness of NUMA topology, so threads routinely compute on tensors that live in another socket's memory and pay remote-access latency for every read.
Approach
We added NUMA-aware tensor parallelism to TorchInductor: FX graph passes that shard weights per socket, and a Triton-CPU launcher that pins each thread to the socket holding the data it operates on. The key move was recognizing that Megatron-LM's tensor parallelism — designed for multi-GPU clusters — is the right abstraction for a single multi-socket machine, after months spent on a partitioning formulation that did not generalize.
Result
Up to 2.63× prefill speedup on dual-socket and 2.27× on four-socket systems, measured on GPT-2, BERT and Gemma 3, with no changes to model code. Accepted at HiPC 2026.

Technologies: MLIR · TorchInductor · Triton-CPU · Python · OpenMP · libnuma · Intel Advisor / VTune

TBT-Former

Learning Temporal Boundary Distributions for Action Localization

Aug 2025 – Nov 2025

Individual research · advised by Prof. Uthayasanker Thayasivam (University of Moratuwa)

Problem
Transformer-based action localizers regress a single deterministic timestamp per boundary, implicitly assuming one exact location. When an action begins or ends gradually, the annotated boundary is only one of several plausible frames, so near-identical moments receive conflicting regression targets and the model is penalized even for reasonable predictions.
Approach
I first ruled out capacity as the bottleneck — scaling the Transformer and swapping in Mamba and convolutional backbones gave only marginal gains. The real fault was the output representation. Borrowing from how Generalized Focal Loss handles occluded box edges in object detection, I designed a head that predicts a discrete distribution over boundary offsets, trained with Distribution Focal Loss. The boundary is decoded as the distribution's expectation; its sharpness encodes the model's uncertainty.
Result
TBT-Former consistently outperformed ActionFormer (ECCV 2022) on THUMOS14 and EPIC-Kitchens 100. In ablations, the boundary-distribution head alone accounted for the largest share of the improvement. Accepted as a short paper at VISAPP 2026; withdrawn before publication as I could not fund registration and travel. Preprint and code remain open.

Technologies: PyTorch · ActionFormer · Mamba · Distribution Focal Loss · THUMOS14 · EPIC-Kitchens 100

Noise-Robust ECG Biometrics

Person Identification from Single-Channel ECG

Sep 2024 – Nov 2024

CS3501 Data Science and Engineering Project · advised by Prof. Uthayasanker Thayasivam · graded A+

Problem
Wearable single-channel ECG could support continuous person identification, but published models exceed 95% accuracy only on denoised gel-electrode recordings. They degrade sharply on the raw, noisy signals a wrist-worn dry electrode actually produces.
Approach
Unable to collect noisy dry-electrode recordings in time, I approximated that noise with randomized synthetic distortions and trained a Siamese 1D convolutional encoder with contrastive loss, mapping clean and noisy frames of the same person to nearby 512-dimensional embeddings.
Result
Under synthetic noise, published models lost 18.3 percentage points of identification accuracy on average across MIT-BIH, PTB Diagnostic and St. Petersburg, while my model lost only 6.1 — without the heavy preprocessing pipeline the baselines depend on.

Technologies: PyTorch · Siamese networks · contrastive learning · MIT-BIH · PTB Diagnostic · St. Petersburg INCART

Hardware & Dataflow Experiments

Dynamically Scheduled Elastic Circuits in VHDL

Independent study in high-level synthesis

I built a dataflow circuit from a hand-written handshake component library and measured it against a static FSM baseline, then compared it with Dynamatic's output for the same C source and benchmarked three buffer-placement algorithms. The exercise is what drew me to dynamically scheduled circuits as a research direction: the gap between what a static schedule must assume and what a dataflow circuit can discover at runtime is exactly the compiler question I want to work on.

Technologies: VHDL · GHDL · Dynamatic · Python
